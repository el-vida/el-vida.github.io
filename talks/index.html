Spiking neural networks

4 animations

AdLIF: a LIF neuron with a brake

The adaptive LIF neuron adds one slow variable w that acts as a brake: every output spike charges it, it leaks slowly, and it subtracts from the drive. Three consequences, each shown against a plain LIF under identical input. Under constant drive the LIF fires like a metronome while the AdLIF's rhythm stretches (spike-frequency adaptation). Under input steps the AdLIF answers at the changes and falls quiet during sustained levels, which is why adaptation helps for speech onsets. And below threshold the coupling makes the membrane resonate: a kicked membrane rebounds below rest, and the frequency response is band-pass where the LIF is low-pass, with a preferred input rhythm.

Blue: membrane potential. Pink: the adaptation variable w. Green: output spikes. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Where noise enters a spiking neuron

Three places to inject noise into the same LIF neuron, and three different behaviors. Input noise rides in with the features and is filtered by the synapse and membrane. Membrane noise enters the state directly and accumulates through the recurrence. Threshold noise (escape noise) leaves the membrane trace completely clean and randomizes only the Bernoulli spike draw near threshold. All three panels run the same input spike train; the gray underlay is the deterministic reference, and three trials of output spikes show each locus's signature.

Orange: input noise. Pink: membrane noise. Yellow: threshold noise. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Noisy vs deterministic spiking neurons

Why the surrogate gradient is not a hack. The opening feeds the same input and membrane trace to both neurons: the deterministic one spikes identically on every trial, the escape-noise one differently each time, equivalently a threshold redrawn from a distribution at every step. A deterministic neuron's spike is a hard step with no usable derivative, so a smooth surrogate is imposed by hand. Make the neuron stochastic and the trials average into a smooth expected output whose exact derivative is that same bump, with the surrogate width playing the role of the noise scale. After Gygax and Zenke (2025), Fig. 1C,D.

White: deterministic step and identical trials. Blue: imposed surrogate. Yellow: escape-noise trials, their mean, and the exact gradient. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

LIF neuron dynamics

A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron driven by a random input spike train: the membrane potential integrates each incoming spike, leaks between them, and emits an output spike with a reset whenever it reaches the threshold.

Blue: membrane potential. Orange: input spikes and threshold. Green: output spikes. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Speech recognition

The ASR pipeline

From sound to text on a real utterance: TIMIT's SA1 sentence. The raw 16 kHz waveform is cut into 25 ms windows with a 10 ms hop; a cursor sweeps the signal while the actual 80-band log-mel spectrogram (computed from this recording) builds up column by column. The features then flow through the standard chain: encoder, per-frame phone posteriors, CTC decoding, and out comes the sentence's true transcript.

Waveform and spectrogram are computed from the real recording; the posterior stage is schematic. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

CTC on the word "see"

How CTC turns per-frame predictions into a transcript without knowing the alignment. One alignment of ten frames collapses by the two rules (merge repeats, then drop the blank ∅); many alignments give the same word, and CTC sums the probability of all of them, organized as paths through a lattice of letters interleaved with blanks. The double "e" shows why the blank exists: the skip between two identical letters is forbidden, or they would merge. Layout after Hannun (2017).

Yellow: one valid path through the lattice. Gray: other paths in the sum. Red: the forbidden same-letter skip. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Generative models and inference

5 animations

What "intractable" means

The evidence integral in one dimension falls to twenty Riemann rectangles. In d dimensions the same grid costs 20d evaluations, a straight line on a log axis that crosses the number of atoms in the observable universe (about 1080) near d = 62 and reaches 10130 at the size of a small VAE latent. The second villain: in high dimension the probability mass hides in a tiny region that no grid point hits. What stays easy is evaluating the joint at a single point, and that pointwise query is exactly the resource that variational inference and MCMC spend wisely.

Red: the exponential grid cost. Yellow: the atoms-in-the-universe line and where the mass hides. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

MAP estimation on a coin

Three flips, three heads: maximum likelihood concludes the coin always lands heads. Multiplying in a near-fair Beta prior and taking the new peak gives the MAP estimate, a sane 0.64. Then the trap: two posteriors with the same mode but very different widths, one loose belief and one locked in, are indistinguishable to MAP, because a point estimate throws away the shape. The closing identity reads MAP as regularized maximum likelihood: a Gaussian prior is weight decay.

White: likelihood. Orange: prior. Blue and pink: posteriors. Green: the MAP estimate. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

KL divergence

The price of believing q when p is true. A live readout shows the KL growing nonlinearly as q slides away from p; the number is then unpacked as a weighted sum, green-minus-blue signed area of q log(q/p). Swapping the arguments changes the price (1.17 vs 1.82 for the same pair), so KL is not a distance. The finale fits a Gaussian to a bimodal target in both directions: minimizing KL(q‖p) hugs one mode (zero-forcing, the variational choice), minimizing KL(p‖q) spans everything (mass-covering).

Blue: p. Green: q. The two finale panels show the same target fitted under the two KL directions. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Variational inference and the ELBO

Why the posterior is out of reach (the evidence integral), and what to do about it. On a bimodal 1D posterior, the deterministic classics come first: MAP is a single point, and Laplace wraps a Gaussian around that point's curvature; both are blind to everything away from the mode. Then variational inference turns the problem into optimization: a Gaussian q is fitted by real gradient ascent on the ELBO, and a bar climbs toward the fixed evidence line log p(x) while the gap, which is exactly KL(q ‖ p), shrinks. The run ends honestly: q locks onto the nearer, smaller mode and the gap stays open, because a single Gaussian cannot match a mixture and the KL direction is zero-forcing. The last slide rearranges the same identity into the reconstruction-minus-KL form that trains VAEs and diffusion models.

Blue: the true posterior. Red: MAP and the KL gap. Orange: Laplace. Green: the variational q and the ELBO. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

DDPM on MNIST

The two halves of a denoising diffusion model, on real MNIST digits. The forward process corrupts a digit with Gaussian noise step by step; every frame is an exact sample of the closed-form marginal q(x t | x 0) under a linear schedule with T = 1000. The reverse pass then plays three trajectories backwards, three noise samples resolving into three different digits, as an illustration of the learned denoiser pθ (no trained model in the loop).

Forward: exact DDPM marginals on a real digit. Reverse: the same trajectories reversed, labeled as illustration. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).
