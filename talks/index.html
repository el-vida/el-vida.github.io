Spiking neural networks

4 animations

AdLIF: a LIF neuron with a brake

The adaptive LIF neuron adds one slow variable w that acts as a brake: every output spike charges it, it leaks slowly, and it subtracts from the drive. Three consequences, each shown against a plain LIF under identical input. Under constant drive the LIF fires like a metronome while the AdLIF's rhythm stretches (spike-frequency adaptation). Under input steps the AdLIF answers at the changes and falls quiet during sustained levels, which is why adaptation helps for speech onsets. And below threshold the coupling makes the membrane resonate: a kicked membrane rebounds below rest, and the frequency response is band-pass where the LIF is low-pass, with a preferred input rhythm.

Blue: membrane potential. Pink: the adaptation variable w. Green: output spikes. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Where noise enters a spiking neuron

Three places to inject noise into the same LIF neuron, and three different behaviors. Input noise rides in with the features and is filtered by the synapse and membrane. Membrane noise enters the state directly and accumulates through the recurrence. Threshold noise (escape noise) leaves the membrane trace completely clean and randomizes only the Bernoulli spike draw near threshold. All three panels run the same input spike train; the gray underlay is the deterministic reference, and three trials of output spikes show each locus's signature.

Orange: input noise. Pink: membrane noise. Yellow: threshold noise. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Noisy vs deterministic spiking neurons

Why the surrogate gradient is not a hack. The opening feeds the same input and membrane trace to both neurons: the deterministic one spikes identically on every trial, the escape-noise one differently each time, equivalently a threshold redrawn from a distribution at every step. A deterministic neuron's spike is a hard step with no usable derivative, so a smooth surrogate is imposed by hand. Make the neuron stochastic and the trials average into a smooth expected output whose exact derivative is that same bump, with the surrogate width playing the role of the noise scale. After Gygax and Zenke (2025), Fig. 1C,D.

White: deterministic step and identical trials. Blue: imposed surrogate. Yellow: escape-noise trials, their mean, and the exact gradient. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Speech and audio

7 animations

From a waveform to a spectrogram

What the Fourier transform actually does, and what a spectrogram column really is. A pure tone is just a list of numbers, and nothing in that list says "440 hertz", so the DFT asks the signal one question at a time: multiply it by a probe wave and average. For the matching probe the product never changes sign and the average survives at 0.500; for probes at 200 and 880 Hz it cancels to 0.004 and 0.002. Doing that at every frequency is the spectrum, and two added tones show up as two peaks whose heights are in the ratio 2.08, the ratio they were mixed at. The FFT computes the same answer with N log N multiplies instead of N²: 341× fewer at N = 4096, and a measured 940× faster in wall-clock here. Since speech changes, the signal is cut into 25 ms windows every 10 ms and one FFT is run per window; the resulting spectrum, stood on end, is one column of the spectrogram, which is where the harmonic stripes and consonant smears come from. The closing experiment is the uncertainty tradeoff, run on two tones 100 Hz apart: a 5 ms window merges them into one peak, a 50 ms window resolves two.

All correlations, peak ratios, timings and the window experiment are computed in the scene; the spectrogram is the real TIMIT utterance. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

The Conformer, step by step

The standard speech encoder since Gulati et al. (2020), built up on real TIMIT features. The finale puts the two connectivity matrices side by side: attention reaches all pairs, convolution a band, and the block gives every frame both views.

Convolution as feature extraction

Why CNNs are the standard audio front end, on real TIMIT audio. A 15-tap kernel slides along the waveform while its response builds below: convolution is one small dot product repeated at every position, y[n] = Σ w[k] x[n+k]. Two hand-set kernels (a moving average and a 150 Hz band-pass) pull different structure out of the same signal: a conv layer is a filterbank, and a trained one learns its own band-passes (SincNet). The same idea in 2D turns the log-mel spectrogram into a temporal-edge map where onsets light up, an exact Sobel convolution of the real spectrogram. Stacking grows the receptive field as r = 1 + L(k-1), and with stride the span compounds: four blocks see about 0.3 s of speech. The closing count: a dense map between two such grids costs 138M weights, 64 shared 3×3 kernels cost 576.

Orange: real waveform. Blue and purple: exact filter responses. Green: the 2D edge map. Kernels are hand-set and labeled as such. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

CTC: the rule, then a trained model's own output

How CTC turns per-frame predictions into a transcript without knowing the alignment. One alignment of ten frames collapses by the two rules (merge repeats, then drop the blank ∅); many alignments give the same word, and CTC sums the probability of all of them, organized as paths through a lattice of letters interleaved with blanks. The double "e" shows why the blank exists: the skip between two identical letters is forbidden, or they would merge. Layout after Hannun (2017).

The last three slides drop the toy and use the real per-frame posteriors of a spiking CTC network trained on TIMIT, run on the same utterance as the pipeline animation above, which the model never saw during training. The famous peakiness is measured rather than drawn: the blank holds 64% of the probability mass on average and wins 66% of the 308 frames, and only 33 short bursts carry the phones. Collapsing the frame-wise argmax and aligning it against the reference by edit distance shows where a greedy decode without a language model succeeds (sh iy for "she", g r iy z iy w aa sh for "greasy wash") and where it substitutes or drops.

Yellow: one valid path through the lattice, and the real phone peaks. Gray: other paths, and the blank probability. Green and red: matches and errors in the alignment. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

LSTM gating and long contexts

Why plain recurrence forgets and gates remember. Gradients through an RNN are products of step-to-step Jacobians, and on a log axis powers are straight lines: 0.960 = 0.002 dies, 1.160 = 304 explodes, and only slopes near zero survive. The LSTM gives memory its own highway, ct = ft·ct-1 + it·(new content), and the whole fix sits in one derivative: dct/dct-1 = ft, a number the network sets at every step from the data rather than a fixed weight, so f = 0.99 still delivers 0.55 of the gradient after 60 steps (the constant error carousel). A store/hold/erase demo with hand-set gates, labeled as such, writes a token, holds it flat for 40 steps, and erases it on command. The spiking connection: an LIF membrane is the fixed-leak special case f = α = e-Δt/τ.

Orange: the fading plain RNN. Purple: the gated cell state. All decay curves are the actual powers. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Griffin-Lim, and getting audio back from a spectrogram

The inverse problem, which every speech synthesiser ends up facing. The STFT produces complex numbers; a spectrogram keeps the magnitude and throws the phase away, so a vocoder has to invent the missing half. Random phase fails badly, 65% off the target magnitudes, because overlapping windows have to agree with each other. Griffin-Lim bounces between the two things we know, inverse STFT then STFT again, each time putting the target magnitude back and keeping whatever phase came out. Measured on real speech it goes from −3.7 dB to −23.7 dB over 100 iterations and then crawls, while the true phase would give −152 dB: it is a local optimum, not the answer. The honest result is the striking part: the reconstructed waveform correlates 0.015 with the original, essentially not at all, yet its spectrogram is indistinguishable, which is exactly why Griffin-Lim sounds metallic. On mel spectrograms it is worse still, since 80 bands from 257 bins is not invertible and the pseudo-inverse costs 15.8% error before the iterations even start, which is the setting of the Spiking Digit Jukebox. The last slide places the alternatives: phase-gradient integration, WaveNet and HiFi-GAN, and the diffusion vocoders that reuse the denoiser from the diffusion animations.

Convergence, the random-phase baseline, the waveform correlation and the mel pseudo-inverse error are all measured on the real utterance. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys) · listen: Spiking Digit Jukebox.

BERT, GPT, and what the mask decides

The same attention three times over, separated only by which entries of the mask are allowed. BERT lets every token see every other one and trains by hiding about 15% of them, so your is rebuilt from she had and dark suit at once. GPT forbids looking ahead, and that triangular mask is what makes left-to-right generation possible at the cost of never seeing the right context. The encoder-decoder pair adds cross-attention, queries from the decoder and keys and values from the encoder, which is the shape of a sequence-to- sequence speech recogniser. The last slide is the trade and the way around it: a masked diffusion language model is BERT run repeatedly, which buys bidirectional context and generation together at the price of many passes, on the same five tokens the diffusion-language-model animation corrupts.

Generative models and inference

What "intractable" means

The evidence integral in one dimension falls to twenty Riemann rectangles. In d dimensions the same grid costs 20d evaluations, a straight line on a log axis that crosses the number of atoms in the observable universe (about 1080) near d = 62 and reaches 10130 at the size of a small VAE latent. The second villain: in high dimension the probability mass hides in a tiny region that no grid point hits. What stays easy is evaluating the joint at a single point, and that pointwise query is exactly the resource that variational inference and MCMC spend wisely.

Red: the exponential grid cost. Yellow: the atoms-in-the-universe line and where the mass hides. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

MAP estimation on a coin

Three flips, three heads: maximum likelihood concludes the coin always lands heads. Multiplying in a near-fair Beta prior and taking the new peak gives the MAP estimate, a sane 0.64. Then the trap: two posteriors with the same mode but very different widths, one loose belief and one locked in, are indistinguishable to MAP, because a point estimate throws away the shape. The closing identity reads MAP as regularized maximum likelihood: a Gaussian prior is weight decay.

White: likelihood. Orange: prior. Blue and pink: posteriors. Green: the MAP estimate. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

KL divergence

The price of believing q when p is true. A live readout shows the KL growing nonlinearly as q slides away from p; the number is then unpacked as a weighted sum, green-minus-blue signed area of q log(q/p). Swapping the arguments changes the price (1.17 vs 1.82 for the same pair), so KL is not a distance. The finale fits a Gaussian to a bimodal target in both directions: minimizing KL(q‖p) hugs one mode (zero-forcing, the variational choice), minimizing KL(p‖q) spans everything (mass-covering).

Blue: p. Green: q. The two finale panels show the same target fitted under the two KL directions. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Variational inference and the ELBO

Why the posterior is out of reach (the evidence integral), and what to do about it. On a bimodal 1D posterior, the deterministic classics come first: MAP is a single point, and Laplace wraps a Gaussian around that point's curvature; both are blind to everything away from the mode. Then variational inference turns the problem into optimization: a Gaussian q is fitted by real gradient ascent on the ELBO, and a bar climbs toward the fixed evidence line log p(x) while the gap, which is exactly KL(q ‖ p), shrinks. The run ends honestly: q locks onto the nearer, smaller mode and the gap stays open, because a single Gaussian cannot match a mixture and the KL direction is zero-forcing. The last slide rearranges the same identity into the reconstruction-minus-KL form that trains VAEs and diffusion models.

Blue: the true posterior. Red: MAP and the KL gap. Orange: Laplace. Green: the variational q and the ELBO. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

DDPM on MNIST and spoken digits

The two halves of a denoising diffusion model, on real data twice over. First on MNIST: the forward process corrupts a digit with Gaussian noise step by step, every frame an exact sample of the closed-form marginal q(x t | x 0) under a linear schedule with T = 1000, and the reverse pass plays three trajectories backwards, three noise samples resolving into three different digits. Then the same recipe on speech: a 64×64 log-mel patch of a real spoken digit (Heidelberg Digits) dissolves into noise and re-emerges, which is exactly the setting of the Spiking Digit Jukebox demo, where a class-conditional diffusion model trained on such patches generates new spoken digits you can listen to. Reverse passes here are illustrations (forward played backwards, no trained model in the loop); the jukebox plays the real thing.

DDPM vs DDIM: one model, two samplers

After training, a diffusion model is one function, the noise predictor; sampling is how you use it. On the same two-Gaussian toy as the flow-matching scene the score is closed form, so both samplers run with the exact optimal predictor and every path is a true simulation. DDPM redraws fresh noise at each of 400 steps: from one fixed starting point, three runs land in two different modes, and the true odds from that exact start are 38:62 (computable in closed form). DDIM deletes the noise term, nothing is retrained, and the same start now follows a single smooth path of length 1.05 where the stochastic walk wandered 47.5. Deterministic paths can be leapt along: 10 steps land within 0.08 on average of the 400-step answer. And the start becomes a code: a small disc of starts maps under DDIM to a tighter disc (0.30 across going in, 0.14 coming out) while DDPM scatters the same disc 4 vs 10 across both modes. A knob η blends the two samplers; η = 0 traces the probability-flow ODE, the bridge to flow matching.

Blue: DDPM walks. Purple: the DDIM path. Yellow: the 10-step leaps. The few-step failure of stochastic sampling with a learned network is stated as a cited claim, not simulated. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Predicting noise, or predicting v

After the schedule is fixed there is still a choice: the network can be asked for the noise ε, for the clean image x₀, or for v = √ᾱ ε − √(1−ᾱ) x₀. On paper they are equivalent; in practice the choice decides what the loss teaches. Measured on real MNIST with the standard 1000-step schedule: at t = 999 the input and the ε-target are correlated 1.000, so simply copying the input scores MSE 0.00004 against 1.00 for predicting zeros, and the loss has almost nothing left to teach. Sampling makes it worse, because recovering x₀ divides by √ᾱ, a 157× error amplification at the last step, which is why samplers clip. The v-target avoids both: its coefficients never exceed one, and copying the input scores 1.92, worse than predicting zeros. The closing picture says it in one frame: at t = 999 the input row and the ε row are the same noise, while the v row is still a crisp digit.

Blue: the ε target. Purple: the v target. All correlations, MSEs and the amplification curve are computed on real MNIST. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Stable Diffusion, and how it relates to DDPM

Latent diffusion in four moves. First the bill: 512×512×3 is 786,432 numbers per denoising step, and attention over pixels would compare 262,144 positions, 69 billion pairs. Then the observation, shown as a real DCT truncation of a real digit: keeping the largest 100 of 784 frequencies is 8× smaller at RMSE 0.177 and still plainly a three. Then the honest limit: at the 48× ratio Stable Diffusion actually uses, a fixed transform collapses to RMSE 0.442, which is exactly why the model trains an autoencoder instead. After that nothing is new: the same DDPM equations run on a 64×64×4 latent, the prompt enters through cross-attention (queries from the latent patches, keys and values from the text encoder), classifier-free guidance exaggerates the difference between the conditional and unconditional predictions, and a DDIM-style solver finishes in 20 to 50 steps.

The compression panels are exact DCT truncations of the digit shown; the pipeline diagrams are schematic. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Flow matching vs diffusion

Generation as transport: carry noise samples to the data. Diffusion does it with a stochastic walk; here the data is a two-Gaussian mixture, whose diffused score is closed form, so the wiggly reverse-SDE trajectories are exact simulations with no trained model anywhere (mean path length about 31 units for a 2.5-unit trip). Flow matching draws the straight line instead: xt = (1−t)x 0 + t x 1, constant velocity. The payoff slide integrates the curved probability-flow ODE with 4 Euler steps (several samples visibly miss the data) while a straight path is exact at any step count. Same framework underneath; diffusion is one choice of path (Lipman et al., 2023).

Blue: exact reverse-SDE trajectories. Green: straight conditional paths. Red: 4-step Euler endpoints. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Discrete vs continuous diffusion

One pixel against one letter. A continuous state is corrupted by tiny Gaussian nudges (the exact DDPM forward chain, plotted for a single coordinate); a discrete token moves by sudden Markov jumps through a finite alphabet (a piecewise-constant path). The same contrast plays out on real data, an MNIST digit dissolving next to a word whose letters get replaced, and it changes what the reverse model must output: a direction in space (score or velocity) versus a distribution over symbols (transition probabilities, trained by cross-entropy).

Blue: continuous sample paths. Yellow: token jump paths. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Diffusion language models: uniform vs masked

Two ways to gradually destroy a sentence, run on five real tokens. Uniform corruption lets any token become any word, again and again; masked (absorbing) corruption only removes: once a token becomes [MASK] it stays there. Reading the two histories upward is generation, and the difference jumps out: the uniform reverse revisits the same slot repeatedly, the masked reverse decides each token exactly once, filling blanks in any order like BERT-style infilling (illustrated with the known sentence; a trained model predicts each fill). Masked is today's workhorse; both are discrete diffusion with different corruption kernels.

Stochastic processes

4 animations

Ito's lemma: why stochastic calculus adds a term

Ordinary calculus works because a smooth curve becomes a straight line when you zoom in. Brownian motion never straightens: zoom into a real path and it looks the same, and the two sums measured along it explain why. The total length diverges (6.6, then 24.8, then 79.8 as the steps get finer), while the sum of squared increments converges on the elapsed time (0.731, 0.976, 0.997 against T = 1). That is the whole content of (dW)² = dt, so the Taylor expansion keeps a second-order term and the chain rule gains ½f″dt. The check is done on the same path: WT² minus 2∫W dW comes out 0.992, and it should be T = 1. The cost of ignoring it is shown on geometric Brownian motion, where the mean finishes at 111 but the median at 102 and 58% of paths end below the average: that gap is the −σ²/2 correction.

Blue: a real Brownian path. Green: convergent squared increments. Orange: divergent length and the GBM ending. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Brownian motion with a spring attached, and the process that quietly underlies both halves of this page. One particle first: the mean relaxes as x₀e−θt with a half-life of 0.46 s, simulated 1.182 against 1.181 predicted. Then four thousand of them, settling into a stationary Gaussian of variance σ²/2θ, measured 0.222 against 0.213. Its memory decays exponentially, and the measured autocorrelations match theory to three decimals (0.861, 0.741, 0.549). The last slide is the punchline: a leaky membrane driven by noisy input is an OU process, and so is the DDPM forward chain, whose 400-step simulation reproduces the OU closed form (mean +0.228 against +0.225, variance 0.990 against 0.983).

Blue: OU paths. Green: the stationary distribution. Purple: the autocorrelation. All comparisons are simulation against closed form. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

Langevin dynamics: noise turns optimisation into sampling

How to draw samples from a distribution when all you have is the slope of its log-density, which is exactly the situation a diffusion model is in. Following the slope alone collapses 220 particles onto 2 points, the modes: that finds peaks, not proportions. Adding noise of precisely the right size turns the same update into a sampler, and the histogram then reproduces the density including the relative weights, 0.364 of the mass in the smaller mode against 0.365 exact. Watching a single particle for a long time shows the same thing in time rather than in ensemble, with 81 crossings between the modes in this run, and it also shows why sampling is slow when modes sit far apart. That difficulty is what annealing over many noise levels fixes, which is the step from Langevin to score-based diffusion.

Orange: noiseless ascent. Blue: Langevin particles and their histogram. Purple: one long chain. The score is closed form, so every path is exact. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).

The Schrodinger bridge

Given where a cloud of particles starts and where it ends up, what did it most likely do in between? Plain diffusion is the right physics with the wrong ending: run it forward and 0.91 of the mass lands in the wrong place. The bridge is the process closest to that reference, in KL, which still respects both marginals, and it is computed here exactly rather than sketched: Sinkhorn alternately fixes the start and the end, and the two marginal errors fall to 10−16. The paths shown are true draws from it, an endpoint sampled from the coupling followed by a Brownian bridge, and from a single starting point the mass splits 0.50 / 0.50 between the two targets. One knob controls everything: as the reference noise shrinks the conditional spread falls 0.83, 0.58, 0.23 and the bridge becomes optimal transport, the straight-line picture of flow matching; with pure noise at one end it is the diffusion forward process instead.

Green: the start distribution. Yellow: the target. Purple: true bridge samples. Orange: unconstrained diffusion, for comparison. Present as full-screen deck (advance with arrow keys).
